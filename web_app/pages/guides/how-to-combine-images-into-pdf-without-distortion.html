How to Combine Images into a PDF Without Stretching or Distorting

Converting photos, scanned receipts, and graphics into a consolidated PDF often results in ugly stretched images or awkward black bars. Learn the math behind aspect-ratio-preserving page fitting.

⏱️ 5 min read 📅 Updated October 2026 🎯 Intermediate

The Distortion Problem: Fixed Page Bounding Boxes

Most images have non-standard aspect ratios. A smartphone photo may have a 4:3 or 16:9 ratio, while a panoramic photo might be 21:9, and a scanned document might be approximately 1:1.414 (the square root of 2, standard for ISO paper).

When you combine these disparate images into a PDF with a fixed paper size (such as standard A4: 595.28 × 841.89 points), basic conversion tools make a critical mistake: they force the image width to equal pageWidth and the image height to equal pageHeight. This immediately stretches or squashes the image, making text unreadable and faces distorted.

The Mathematical Solution: Aspect-Preserving Containment

To render an image naturally on a PDF page without distortion, the conversion engine must calculate an aspect-ratio-locked bounding box:

Bounding Box Calculation Formula

Let available printable width be Wavail = PageWidth - 2 × Margin and printable height be Havail = PageHeight - 2 × Margin.

ImageAspect = ImageWidth / ImageHeight
PageAspect = Wavail / Havail

• If ImageAspect > PageAspect (image is wider than page):
    DrawWidth = Wavail
    DrawHeight = Wavail / ImageAspect
• Else (image is taller than page):
    DrawHeight = Havail
    DrawWidth = Havail × ImageAspect

Centering offsets: X = Margin + (Wavail - DrawWidth) / 2, Y = Margin + (Havail - DrawHeight) / 2.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does converting images to PDF re-compress and degrade quality?
PNG files are embedded natively as lossless flate-encoded image streams without any re-compression. JPEG files are embedded using their native DCT bytes directly, preserving 100% of the original photo quality without generational loss.
Can I combine mixed formats (e.g. 2 JPEGs and 1 PNG)?
Yes. FileWorks handles heterogeneous image batches seamlessly, applying the proper container embedding for each individual file format.
